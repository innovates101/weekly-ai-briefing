OCC Gives Bank Examiners an AI Playbook — Your Program Will Be Judged

Overview

The OCC has released a formal framework for bank examiners to evaluate AI — which the guidance refers to as "super intelligence" — across every aspect of banks' business activities. For national banks, this isn't optional reading; it's the standard by which their AI programs will now be examined.

The Details

  • The OCC released guidance for examiners evaluating AI (“super intelligence”) in banks' business activities — formalizing how bank AI programs will be assessed during regulatory exams.
  • The framework establishes how examiners should approach AI governance, risk management, and controls in regulated institutions.
  • National banks now have a clear regulatory benchmark for what “adequate” AI governance looks like in examiners' eyes.
  • The guidance arrives as AI agent liability concerns are surging — Bank of America warned customers about AI agent liability, US regulators flagged that banking agents are riskier than general-purpose AI, and India's cybercrime agency issued alerts to banks and fintechs, all in the same week.
  • Banks without formal AI governance structures face increased examination risk as examiners begin applying the new framework.

Why It Matters

Every national bank examiner now has a playbook for AI oversight. Banks that have been treating AI governance as optional — running pilots, deploying agents, using models in underwriting — now face structured regulatory scrutiny. The question isn't whether your AI is effective; it's whether your governance would satisfy an OCC examiner.

For risk officers and technology leaders, this is a forcing function. The OCC's framework signals what "adequate" looks like — and banks that formalize their AI risk frameworks before the next exam cycle will be in a meaningfully stronger position than those that don't.

1. Bank of America Warns You May Be Liable for What Your AI Agent Does

🧠 Liability is shifting to users. As banks condition or restrict AI agent access, financial institutions and their customers both need clear frameworks for what agents can do — and who is responsible when things go wrong.

🧠 Open banking meets AI agent friction. Banks blocking third-party AI agents will face tension with open banking mandates — but liability and fraud risks are forcing the industry to establish guardrails before regulators mandate them.

4. Why US Regulators Say a Simple AI Banking Agent Could Be Riskier Than ChatGPT
Coinpaper
US financial regulators argue that narrow, task-focused AI banking agents pose unique systemic risks: unlike general-purpose AI, banking agents have authority to act on users' behalf, meaning errors translate immediately into real financial harm rather than simply producing a wrong answer.
🧠 Authority amplifies risk. A chatbot that gives bad advice is a liability; an AI agent that executes a bad transfer is a catastrophe. Regulators are right to treat agentic AI as a categorically different risk class requiring separate governance frameworks.
